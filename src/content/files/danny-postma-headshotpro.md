Hello again, and welcome to File #010. Thank you for reading.

In the first week of HeadshotPro, in March 2023, the servers kept going down while Danny Postma slept. He'd wake at 3am to check them.

Two months later he took his wife to the Netherlands for two weeks and switched on automatic refunds, so anyone unhappy could get their money back without him. At home, his wife handled the support inbox each morning. The business kept running.

This file is about how he got from the first situation to the second, and about a simple habit that explains most of his success: he looks at what people are typing into Google before he writes a line of code. It's also a slightly unusual file, because Danny stopped sharing revenue figures in 2023. "I don't share revenue anymore," he told an interviewer. "Sorry." So we'll show you what the company says, what others estimate, how far the two agree, and what he has said since things got harder in 2026. (All figures are in US dollars.)

## The short version

- **Who:** Danny Postma, a Dutch founder who has been building small internet products since he was 21, and who sold his first AI company in 2021.
- **What:** HeadshotPro. Upload a handful of selfies, and an AI returns a set of professional-looking headshots in different outfits and backdrops. Individuals pay once. Companies buy them for whole teams.
- **Why it matters:** it shows how far search traffic and affiliates can carry a business. They were the engine at the start, though by 2026 he says a large share of revenue goes on Google ads.
- **The number to remember:** four, the products he built on the new image AI in about six months before one stuck, out of a portfolio of many more.

## The founder

Danny started in 2015, at 21, with a site called Landingfolio: a gallery of well-designed landing pages for people who build websites. It was built on WordPress and, by his own account, it was hard to make money from.

His second act went much better. He built Headlime, a tool that helped people write marketing copy. Then he got into the first batch of outside developers given access to GPT-3, the model that came before ChatGPT, and Headlime became one of the earliest AI writing products anywhere.

He sold it in March 2021, about eight months after launch. Indie Maker List says the buyer was Conversion.ai, the company later renamed Jasper, and the price was "seven figures". By Danny's own account it was over a million dollars. Headlime was making about $20,000 a month at the time.

His reason for selling tells you a lot about him. He told Arvid Kahl on the Bootstrapped Founder podcast that he'd had three options: raise money from investors, keep bootstrapping and hire a team, or sell. Two buyers were interested. And he didn't want to manage people.

## How he chooses what to build

Most founders come up with an idea and then work out how to find customers. Danny works the other way round.

"I only build products where there's SEO," he said on that podcast. SEO is search engine optimisation, the craft of appearing near the top of Google. Before he builds anything he opens Ahrefs, a tool that shows how many people search for a phrase each month and how hard it would be to rank for it. If plenty of people are searching and the competition is weak, he builds. If nobody's searching, he doesn't.

He does it this way, he said in 2023, because he "hated marketing", though he added that he was "getting a little bit better at it". Search traffic is marketing you only have to do once. If your page answers the question someone typed, they arrive on their own, month after month, for free.

## Four ideas in six months

In the late summer of 2022 a model called Stable Diffusion was released. It could generate images from text, and for the first time anyone could build on it. Danny moved straight away. He has described the sequence in a 2023 podcast and a 2024 interview.

1. **Stock AI.** AI-generated stock photos. He liked a domain name for it, but the owner wanted $25,000 to $30,000, so he rented it at about $500 a month with an option to buy. He also worried about the legal risk around image rights. After three months and $1,500 the product hadn't caught on, so he walked away from both.
2. **ProfilePicture.AI.** Built in about 30 hours. It sold well for a week and then slowed.
3. **Deep Agency.** A "virtual modelling agency", announced on 6 March 2023. Journalists loved writing about it, and almost nobody bought it. Eleven days later he called it "quite the failure sales:traffic wise".
4. **HeadshotPro.** Announced on 16 March 2023, ten days after Deep Agency. In his words it was "an instant hit".

Four is a floor, not a full count. In the same period he put out other small AI products, including a tattoo generator and a meme app, and on the 2023 podcast Arvid Kahl counted "16, 17, almost 20" projects on his portfolio site.

It would be easy to conclude that the fourth idea was the clever one. We'd put it differently. He had a way of testing each idea that cost a few weeks and a few hundred dollars, and he was willing to drop three of them. Deep Agency had press coverage and HeadshotPro had sales, and he followed the sales.

He then narrowed further. Competitors were branching into dating-profile photos and cartoon avatars. He stayed with one job: a headshot you could put on LinkedIn or a company website.

## The domain name

He paid $3,000 for headshotpro.com.

For a product with no customers that might seem a lot. His argument is that a strong, obvious .com helps you rank in search and stops a competitor from picking up people who half-remember your name. Remember that a few months earlier he had refused to pay $25,000 for a domain on an idea he hadn't proven. With Stock AI he rented the name and tested the idea first. With HeadshotPro the idea had already worked on a smaller scale, so he bought the name outright.

He also bought some older, related websites, such as profilepicturemaker.com, which already had visitors. He added a banner to each one pointing to HeadshotPro, and so picked up their existing traffic.

## The second engine

Search was the first source of customers. The second was affiliates.

An affiliate programme pays people a commission for every customer they send you. A blogger writes "the 7 best AI headshot tools", links to you with a tracked link, and earns a share of each sale.

He started on 17 March 2023, the day after launch, offering affiliates 20 percent of every sale. HeadshotPro runs its programme through a tool called Rewardful, and Rewardful has published a case study about it. That study says the programme brings in more than $50,000 a month and accounts for over 15 percent of HeadshotPro's revenue. The caveats are that it's written by the vendor, has no date on it and doesn't say how many affiliates there are.

Danny's reasoning is the useful part. Bloggers and reviewers need a reason to write about you, he says, and without a commission, far fewer would. Their articles also link to his site, which helps his search ranking. So the affiliates feed the search traffic as well as bringing in sales themselves.

## The summer it tripled

Then he had some luck. In the summer of 2023 a phone app called Remini went viral with AI-generated professional photos, and for a few weeks it seemed everyone was posting them.

People went to Google and typed "AI headshots", and HeadshotPro was at the top. "My sales have been tripled since that day," he said.

He didn't cause that wave. But he'd spent months making sure that if one came, he would be where it landed.

## What it cost him

That same interview has a section we think is worth reading in full, because founders rarely say these things on the record.

He described being mentally unable to build new features, and going three weeks without making anything. He counted about five serious competitors and said he felt anxious about more arriving. He said he was "really nervous" about launching a plan for company teams. A first attempt at hiring someone in Europe had failed because the time difference ate into his rest.

So in the same months that the business was tripling, the person running it was struggling. Both things were true.

## Solo, and then not

In August 2023 he still called himself a solopreneur, with his wife helping for two or three hours a day. But he had just posted his first job advert, for a machine learning engineer, and was studying how companies like Gumroad and Notion run remote teams.

By early 2024 he had a "small team", starting with contractors and part-timers. He told Unite.AI that fear of hiring and managing people had kept him automating alone for too long, and that he should have built a team sooner. That's the same man who sold Headlime to avoid managing anyone.

Danny describes HeadshotPro as a Singapore-based company. It has never published a headcount. But the website now offers single sign-on, data-processing agreements and SOC 2 Type II security compliance for teams of 1,000 or more, and it names HubSpot's sales team as a customer. That's not what a one-person business usually offers.

The team gave him something Headlime never did. In June 2025 he posted that he had just spent two weeks in Canada without touching work, and the company kept running.

## 2026: a smaller company

In July 2026 Danny broke a long silence on X. He said a Google update, Google's Gemini competing directly and a flood of rivals had cut HeadshotPro's revenue forecasts "by a lot", and that he'd "had to fire half the team". A pivot had failed and "burned many months of work". He'd burned out again, and said that managing people had "sucked the joy away", though he called working with a team "amazing".

He has since cut costs, automated more of the business, launched a new website, and decided it's fine for HeadshotPro to be "done". Revenue, he says, is "stable at still a beautiful number". The same day he posted that he thought "half our revenue now goes to" Google: Gemini for the AI, Google Cloud for storage, and the "rest of revenue" on Google Ads.

The product hasn't stood still. He has since added lifestyle photo shoots and a thumbnail editor. But most of his own time, he says, has gone on a new product, which he says has automated about 95 percent of his coding work. In November he was testing a course with beta users.

## How the product works

In early 2024, Danny said most rivals take Stable Diffusion and retrain it on each customer's photos, and leave it there. He said HeadshotPro ran "dozens" of additional models, some open-source and some its own, and that the team had spent about a year building custom ones. He named two. One checks the photos people upload and filters out anything inappropriate in the results. The other cleans up the warped eyes and odd skin that AI faces are prone to.

About fifteen to seventeen input photos was, he said in 2024, the magic number. The home page now asks for "a few selfies". By 2026 HeadshotPro was running on Google's Gemini models, he says.

The company's own About page is candid on one point. It says the output only became good enough for everyday professional use in early 2025, which is nearly two years after it started selling.

A review by BetterPic, which sells a rival product and says so, rated HeadshotPro 7 out of 10 and reported some distortions, such as blurry eyes and odd fingers, in a share of the images. Bear in mind who wrote it.

## The numbers

|---|---|---|
| Revenue since launch | More than $10 million | The company's About page |
| Customers | More than 250,000 | The company's home page |
| Headshots generated | More than 17 million | The company |
| Satisfaction | 96.7 percent satisfied and 3.3 percent refunded, across 217,879 purchases from 2023 to 2026 | The company |
| Price | $29 for 30 headshots, up to $59 for 70. Team volume discounts of up to 50 percent (the home page says 60) | The company |
| Monthly revenue | About $300,000, as of December 2024 | Starter Story's estimate, from public sources; not confirmed |
| Affiliate programme | More than $50,000 a month, over 15 percent of revenue | Rewardful, the vendor |

Since Danny won't tell us, we did some arithmetic. This is ours and he hasn't confirmed any of it.

If $50,000 is 15 percent of monthly revenue, the total would be about $330,000 a month. That is close to Starter Story's $300,000 estimate. And 217,879 purchases at between $29 and $59 each comes to somewhere between $6 million and $13 million, which is in the range of the $10 million the company claims. It's a loose range: team purchases are priced differently.

These figures are consistent with each other, but they all come from the company, a vendor or an estimate built on public posts, and they predate the fall in revenue forecasts Danny described in July 2026. His only recent word on revenue is that it is "stable at still a beautiful number".

## What made it possible

- **A sale behind him.** The Headlime money meant he could try four ideas without worrying about rent.
- **Years of SEO practice.** Landingfolio, his first site, was itself an exercise in getting found on Google.
- **A cheap way to test ideas,** and a willingness to drop the ones that didn't sell.
- **Narrow focus.** He sold professional headshots and didn't branch out.

> **Try this**
>
> **Search before you build.** Type the thing you want to sell into Google the way a customer would. Use a keyword tool (several have free versions) to see how many people search for it each month. If the answer is almost none, you'll need another way to find customers, so plan it now.
>
> **Run cheap tests with an end date.** Give each idea a deadline and a sales target before you start. Danny rented a domain for $500 a month and stopped paying after three months.
>
> **Follow the sales, not the applause.** One of his ideas got the press and another got the customers. Know which of those you're measuring.
>
> **Pay people to write about you.** If you sell online, set up a simple affiliate programme early. A commission gives reviewers a reason to cover you, and their links help your search ranking.
>
> **Automate the thing that wakes you up.** For him it was refunds. Decide what can be refunded or answered automatically, and write the rule down.

## What could go wrong

**A crowded market.** AI headshots are easy to copy. Rivals claim sharper images, faster delivery and more security certificates.

**Google, twice over.** Danny says a Google search update and Google's own Gemini have already cut HeadshotPro's forecasts, and that roughly half its revenue now goes back to Google in AI, cloud and advertising costs.

**Faces are sensitive.** Customers upload photos of themselves. The company says it doesn't train on them and deletes them within 30 days. Those are its own claims, and the rules on facial data are tightening in many places.

**The founder's energy.** By his own account he has burned out again and his attention has moved to a new product. A business that is "done" still needs someone to notice when it stops working.

**Opacity.** Every number here is the company's own or somebody's estimate. That is his right, and it limits what anyone outside can know.

## What to watch

1. **More published numbers.** Whether the company says anything beyond "$10 million" and "a beautiful number", or anyone outside confirms them.
2. **The smaller team.** Whether the people left after the July cuts can keep the business running as he steps back.
3. **Quality.** Whether its own models keep ahead as everyone else's improve.
4. **The next product.** He says he has spent most of 2026 on a new tool that automates his own coding work, and is testing a course.

He never wanted to manage people. He built a team anyway, found it was what made the business run without him, and then, when revenue forecasts fell, found out what it cost to shrink one.
