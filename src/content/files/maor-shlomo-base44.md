Hello again, and welcome to File #009. Thank you for reading.

On 7 October 2023, Maor Shlomo was called up for reserve duty in the Israeli military. He was 29 or 30, and by any normal measure already a success: he had co-founded a data company in his early twenties and run it as chief executive while it raised about $125 million and grew past a hundred employees.

Shlomo's own account of Base44 starts after his service. On a two-month trip to Thailand and the Philippines with his partner, Yuval, a tattoo artist, he tried to build her a website and a system for managing clients in WordPress. "I was the CEO of a tech company, and yet it was so difficult," he told Calcalist. "That didn't make sense to me." (Remarkable Magazine tells a different story, of a nonprofit his reserve unit helped that was quoted a fortune for simple tools. We couldn't find that in anything Shlomo has said.)

He kept thinking about that. Base44 came out of stealth in early 2025. Once things started clicking, he says, it took three weeks to go from nothing to a rate of a million dollars a year. About four months after launch, Wix bought it.

The headlines said: solo founder, six months, $80 million. Each of those three claims is partly true, and the gaps are the most interesting part of this file. (All figures are in US dollars.)

## The short version

- **Who:** Maor Shlomo, an Israeli founder, previously co-founder and chief executive of the data company Explorium.
- **What:** Base44, a tool that turns a written description into a working app. It is part of a category people have started calling "vibe coding".
- **Why it matters:** it's the best-documented recent case of one person building an AI product alone, with his own money, and selling it fast. Because the buyer is a listed company, we can also see what happened afterwards.
- **The number to remember:** $18 million. That, according to Wix's own annual report, is roughly what was paid in cash at the start. Most of the rest of the money depended on what Base44 did next.

## The founder

Shlomo isn't a newcomer who got lucky. He co-founded Explorium in 2017. Calcalist has put his age then at 24; he says he was 23. It sells tools that help companies find and use outside data, and it was backed by Insight Partners, one of the big American technology investors. He ran it for about seven years.

So he knew exactly what a venture-backed company feels like: the fundraising, the board, the hundred-person payroll. Base44 was a deliberate turn away from all of it.

He stepped down as Explorium's chief executive in late 2024, after a year of reserve duty in the intelligence unit 8200, though he stayed on its board. Calcalist describes Base44 as a side project that began on the trip he took after his service. Yuval, now his wife, later told the same paper that her tattoo business was "simply the trigger", and that he had been thinking for years about how not knowing how to code stops people building software. He has said publicly that he has severe ADHD. And he built Base44 with no co-founder, no investor and, at the start, no employees.

He turned down offers from Israeli venture funds. "Almost every fund operating here approached me, but I wanted to continue bootstrapping," he told Calcalist. He was the only shareholder.

## What Base44 does

You type what you want, such as "an app where my five staff can log their hours and I can approve them", and Base44 builds it. It makes the screens and the database, handles the log-in and puts the result on the internet. You don't see any code unless you ask to.

That's the whole pitch, and it's aimed at people like Yuval: small business owners who need a simple tool and can't afford a developer. Plans ran from about $20 to $200 a month.

He wasn't alone in the idea. Lovable, Bolt and Replit were all doing versions of the same thing, with tens or hundreds of millions of dollars behind them. Shlomo had no investors.

## Three weeks

The first users, by the account he gave on Lenny Rachitsky's podcast, were three friends.

Before things took off, by his own account, he "failed miserably at marketing". What worked was what several founders in this series have done: he posted what he was building, day by day, on LinkedIn and X, including the awkward parts. He published what the AI models were costing him and what he was earning. In a category full of companies losing money on every customer, here was a founder showing his working and a profit.

It spread quickly. In his own words, it took three weeks to go from $0 to $1 million in annualised revenue once growth started, and seven weeks to pass 140,000 users. Remarkable Magazine adds that it reached $1.5 million within four weeks.

Counting the users is harder. Calcalist reported more than 100,000 at the time of the sale; Shlomo later told the same paper 350,000. Remarkable Magazine says 250,000, and the podcast's notes say 400,000. They were counted on different dates and probably in different ways, and we can't tell you which is right. All of them are a lot for a product a few months old.

Two weeks before the deal, Shlomo posted his profit for May 2025, by his own figures: about $189,000. He had forecast $100,000.

## What "solo" meant

The headlines called it a one-man company. At launch that was accurate. By the time Wix announced the deal on 18 June 2025, it wasn't. Calcalist counted six employees, and fewer than ten people in all. On the day of the sale, Shlomo said, there were six employees, two in support and the rest programmers, and all but one had joined 30 days earlier.

So the fair description is that one person built the product and ran it through its first, fastest stretch, then hired a handful of people when it became clear the company would be sold and would have to grow.

He has been honest about what that stretch was like. Speaking at a conference run by EY Israel, he was asked whether it had been lonely. "Yes, absolutely I felt lonely," he said. He also said that being what he called a non-democratic company, one where a single person makes every decision without a meeting, was a huge advantage early on.

Both of those are worth hearing if you're tempted by the solo route. It was fast, and by his own account it was lonely.

How did one person keep up with rivals that had whole engineering departments? Largely by using the same kind of tool he was selling. The notes for his podcast episode say he hadn't written a line of front-end code himself in three months, and the episode is billed around AI writing 90 percent of his code. He told the same EY audience he spends "20-30 percent of my time automating the business rather than writing code".

## The deal, read carefully

Here is the headline: Wix buys Base44 for $80 million.

Wix is an Israeli company, listed in New York, that lets people build websites without code. An AI that builds whole apps from a sentence is either its future or its replacement, so buying Base44 made sense. "There's no better fit," Shlomo said. He told reporters Wix was probably the only company that could help Base44 scale without slowing it down.

Now the detail. Wix's annual report sets out the split: $18.1 million in cash at the start, and earn-out payments it valued at $74.1 million, a total of $92.2 million. Everything after that comes from Wix's quarterly results, as reported by Calcalist and Globes.

So "$80 million in cash" was never accurate. He got a modest sum up front and a promise of much more if the product kept growing. An earn-out is exactly that: the buyer pays later, and only if agreed targets are hit.

It was a good bet for him: the product grew far faster than the targets assumed.

Wix hasn't published the thresholds that trigger each payment. Calcalist reports that they are understood to be set at intervals of $50 million in annual recurring revenue, but says Wix's filings don't confirm it.

## What happened next

- **November 2025.** Wix said Base44 had 2 million users, seven times as many as when the deal closed, with more than 1,000 new paying subscribers a day. Its share of its market had gone from low single digits to more than 10 percent.
- **March 2026.** Wix reported that Base44 had passed $100 million in annualised recurring revenue.
- **May 2026.** Wix said about $150 million as of May.
- **June 2026.** Base44 launched Base 1, its own AI model, built on an open-source model and tuned for building apps.
- **August 2026.** About $200 million, Shlomo said.
- **September 2026.** It launched Base Code, a tool for teams working on existing code, pitched by Calcalist as a rival to Claude Code and Cursor.
- **November 2026.** Wix said Base44 was nearing $250 million in annual recurring revenue. Calcalist put its staff at about 300, against six at the sale.

Less than two years earlier this was a side project with three friends using it.

There's a cost, and it shows up in Wix's own accounts. Every time someone builds an app with Base44, an AI model has to run, and that costs money. Wix said Base44's gross margin was near zero at the start of 2026, meaning it spent almost everything it earned delivering the product. By the third quarter, after the switch to Base 1, Wix put it at 58 percent, and it expects more than 60 percent by the end of the year.

Wix also spent heavily to feed the growth. Its annual report says spending on user acquisition and other marketing rose from $175.6 million to $243.6 million in 2025, "mainly due to Base44", and that many Wix staff were moved onto it. When Wix announced that spending in November 2025, its shares fell more than 15 percent at the open. Calcalist's sharper observation was that Base44's success is masking a slowdown in Wix's original business. In November 2026 Wix said it would put much of the improved margin back into marketing, and it made its president, Nir Zohar, co-chief executive.

So the founder's side project is now, in effect, the main thing Wix is betting on.

## What you can copy, and what you can't

**Copyable:**

- **Start from something that should have been easy and wasn't.** He didn't begin with a technology. He began with a website for his partner's business that a former tech chief executive couldn't build without a struggle.
- **Build in public, including the costs.** His posts about what the AI cost him and what he earned did more than any advert.
- **Be profitable early.** A profit of $189,000 in a month, by his figures, meant he didn't need investors, which meant he could say no to them, which meant he owned all of it when the offer came.
- **Use the tools yourself.** He competed with funded teams by letting AI write most of the code.

**Not copyable:**

- **His track record.** He'd already run a company that raised $125 million. People listened when he posted, and Wix knew who it was dealing with.
- **The timing.** Early 2025 was the first wave of excitement about AI app builders. It's a crowded field now.
- **The buyer.** A large listed company felt threatened by exactly this technology and needed an answer quickly. You can't plan on finding a buyer in that position.

> **Try this**
>
> **Find the job that should have been easy.** What have you tried to do recently, for yourself or someone close to you, that took far longer than it should have? A website, a booking system, a report? Ask whether an AI tool can now do a rough version, and whether other people would pay for that.
>
> **Find your first three users.** His were friends. Name yours before you build, and ask them whether they'd actually use it.
>
> **Work out your cost per customer.** If your product runs an AI model every time someone uses it, calculate what one active customer costs you a month. Base44 grew to $100 million with almost no margin, and it needed Wix's money to do it.
>
> **Read the deal, not the headline.** If you ever sell a business, ask for three numbers in writing: cash on the day, payments that depend on your staying, and payments that depend on results. Assume only the first is certain.

## What could go wrong

**The cost of every answer.** The margin has recovered fast, but it depends on Wix's own model keeping pace with rivals' models, and Wix plans to spend much of the gain on marketing.

**Rivals.** Lovable, Replit, Bolt and others are well funded and moving fast, and the big AI labs can build the same thing. With Base Code, Base44 is now competing with them directly.

**Dependence on one buyer's patience.** Wix is spending hundreds of millions on Base44 while its older business slows. Shareholders have flinched twice: the shares fell more than 15 percent at the open on the November 2025 results and about 31 percent on the May 2026 results, and Wix cut about a fifth of its staff.

**The founder.** He's now an employee, with large payments tied to results. What he says publicly is shaped by that. The earn-out runs on results to the end of 2028, and nobody outside Wix knows what keeps him there after that.

**The numbers we couldn't pin down.** User counts conflict. The early revenue figures are his own. And the later payments are amounts Wix has set aside, not confirmed payouts.

## What to watch

1. **The earn-out.** How much more Wix sets aside, how much is actually paid, and whether it ever publishes the terms.
2. **The margin.** Whether the margin holds above 60 percent while Wix keeps spending on growth.
3. **Wix itself.** Whether Base44 lifts the whole company or just hides the decline of the rest.
4. **Base Code.** Whether a tool for teams with existing code can win customers from Claude Code and Cursor.
5. **Shlomo.** Whether he stays once the earn-out years end, and what he builds if he doesn't.

He tried to build his partner a website and found it absurdly hard, and he built something that could do the job for $20 a month. Then he took a deal that paid him most of the money only if the product kept growing, and it grew far beyond what the headline price assumed.
