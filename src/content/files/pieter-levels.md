Hello again, and welcome to File #008.

Somewhere on a rented server there is a file called index.php. In March 2026 it was 40,870 lines long. If you printed it, you'd get a stack of paper about as thick as two or three novels.

According to the man who wrote it, that one file is the whole of the Photo AI website. There's no team behind it and no tidy folder of well-organised code. He says it took in $105,000 that month and that $80,000 of it was profit.

Professional software engineers tend to wince when they hear this. You aren't supposed to build things this way. And yet the man who did, Pieter Levels, is, in the words of Stripe's co-founder John Collison, "maybe the most prominent indie hacker", meaning someone who builds software businesses on their own, without investors. He has been at it for more than a decade, he tells the internet what he earns as he goes, and he has never taken a cent of investment.

This week we look at how he works, what the numbers say, and which parts are worth borrowing. (All figures are in US dollars, and all of them are his own.)

## The short version

- **Who:** Pieter Levels, a self-taught Dutch developer known online as @levelsio.
- **What:** a portfolio of products he runs alone. Photo AI turns a few selfies into realistic photos of you. Interior AI redesigns a room from a picture. Nomads.com (formerly Nomad List) and Remote OK are older, non-AI sites. In September 2026 he made Nomads.com almost free, at $1 to sign up, to grow its community.
- **Why it matters:** he's the working proof that one person with simple tools can run several profitable products at once, and AI has made that easier for him in three different ways.
- **The number to remember:** 40,870. That was the number of lines in the single file behind Photo AI in March 2026. In July 2023 it was almost 14,000.

## The founder

In early 2014 Pieter was a young Dutchman who had spent most of the previous year travelling round Asia with a backpack, and he had a problem he has described very plainly. He kept starting projects and not finishing them. "I was always scared to launch," he told Wired that summer. He would polish something for months and then drift to the next idea.

So he made a public promise. In March 2014 he announced on his blog that he would launch 12 startups in 12 months. It was a gimmick, and he knew it. Wired wrote about him that August and pointed out, fairly, that these weren't really twelve companies. But the gimmick worked, because a deadline every month left no time for polishing.

One of the twelve was a list of cities, ranked for people who work remotely while travelling: cost of living, internet speed, weather, safety. The first version was a shared Google spreadsheet. He tweeted it, strangers started filling in the cells, and he turned it into a website, Nomad List, which went to number one on Product Hunt. A job board for remote work, Remote OK, followed. Those two sites paid his way for years, long before AI.

He never really stopped launching things. On the Lex Fridman podcast in 2024 he was introduced as having started more than 40 companies. In his 2025 conversation with Collison, the count was "at least 70". Either way, he says the same thing every time he's asked: most ideas don't work.

Along the way he wrote a book about building products this way. He sold $50,000 of pre-orders before he had written a word. He puts that down partly to his name and partly to hard work: about 125,000 tweets in ten years. He has lived in more than 40 countries and worked from a laptop the whole time.

## Why he works alone

He works alone because he prefers it. "I do everything myself," he told Lex Fridman. "I'm a designer, I'm the developer, I make everything."

He has said he doesn't enjoy managing people and would rather be making things. He has also never raised venture capital, though investors have asked. His reasoning is simple. Investor money commits you to a path where you either become enormous or you fail, and the odds of becoming enormous are a few percent. He would rather, he says, own a company that makes $10 million a year outright.

Collison's summary of the result was that these are "just Pieter Levels productions".

## The stack

His choice of tools is unfashionable.

He described it on the Fridman podcast as "vanilla HTML, jQuery, PHP and SQLite". If those names mean nothing to you, the short version is that they are some of the oldest, plainest tools for building websites. Most professional developers moved on from them years ago to newer, more elaborate systems called frameworks.

Pieter didn't move on, and he's cheerful about why. Partly it was an accident: he says he never found the time to learn the newer tools. Partly it's conviction. PHP, he says, "just stays the same and works", while frameworks take "so much work to just maintain". He calls his suspicion of framework fashion "my most controversial take".

Photo AI is the extreme case. By his own post, the entire website is one PHP file. He has run sites this way before. Remote OK was famously one file too.

We aren't going to tell you that's a good idea in general. For most teams it isn't. But for one person it has a real advantage, and it's the same one that runs through this whole story. Nobody else needs to understand the code, and he can hold all of it in his head. Nothing has to be explained or handed over, and no update can break it while he's on a beach.

## The AI products

When image-generating AI arrived in 2022, Pieter treated it the way he treats everything. He launched something small and watched whether people paid.

Interior AI came first: upload a photo of your living room and see it restyled. On the Fridman podcast he said it reached about $10,000 a month within a week and was making "forty, fifty" thousand a month two years in. His X bio now puts it at $23,000 a month.

Then came an app that made AI avatars from your selfies, which were everywhere on social media for a few weeks in late 2022. The app, AvatarAI.me, sold $100,000 of avatars in its first ten days, he posted at the time (2,943 sales at about $33 each). He later recalled it as about $150,000 in a week and "ten k a day" at the peak. Then the craze passed, and bigger companies with iPhone apps, such as Lensa, moved in.

What he did next is the useful part. He didn't cling to the avatar app, and he didn't throw it away either. He turned it into Photo AI, a steadier product built on the same idea: train a model on your face, then produce photos of you in any setting or outfit, good enough to replace a photographer for a profile picture or a product shot. He charges a monthly subscription for it.

There are three things to notice.

**The numbers go down as well as up.** His one-day peak, after the Lex Fridman podcast in September 2024, ran at $161,000 a month. In March 2026 he reported $105,000 for the month, and his X bio now says $68,000. Interior AI is down to $23,000. Newer lines have made up some of the gap, chiefly Vibe Jam, a sponsored game competition he runs, at $44,000 a month. Anyone who quotes you a single Pieter Levels figure without a date is leaving something out.

**The margin is very high.** $80,000 of profit on $105,000 of revenue is about 76 percent. He has since published his costs. In November 2026 he said Photo AI's GPU bill had fallen from $36,000 a month to $7,000, and that everything else he runs costs about $300 a month. So the 76 percent margin from March is probably higher now, though he hasn't given a new Photo AI profit figure.

**Not everything in circulation adds up.** In the Stripe interview itself, Collison's introduction puts Remote OK at $3.4 million in revenue, more than the $3.1 million a year he gives for everything, so that is probably a lifetime figure. We've also seen it claimed that he wants to sell Photo AI and Interior AI. We couldn't find him saying so; his fund page still lists both as holdings. In July 2025 he said he keeps old projects running rather than shutting them down. One of them still earns about $50 a month on code he hasn't touched in ten years.

## Three ways he uses AI

People tend to lump these together, and they're quite different.

**AI is the product.** Photo AI and Interior AI are, underneath, other people's image models with a well-made product wrapped around them. He didn't invent the technology. What he did was package it quickly and price it sensibly.

**AI helps him build.** In February 2025, by his own account never having made a game, he asked Cursor, an AI coding tool, for a flight simulator. About three hours later it was running in a web browser, and it was multiplayer about eight hours in. It's at fly.pieter.com. He says it reached a $1 million annual run rate in 17 days. That is a run rate, not a year's takings. One gaming site reported $87,000 in the first month, from in-game advertising and sales of virtual planes, and at least one blog says the income later fell away. He then ran a competition, Vibe Jam, for games built the same way. The first drew 1,170 entries, and a second followed in 2026. Vibe Jam is now sponsored, and his bio puts it at $44,000 a month, his second-biggest line. On 1 August 2026 he posted that he had "vibecoded" a full video editor into Photo AI, meaning he described what he wanted and let the AI write it. He also invested in Cursor in 2025, and his fund page records that stake as exited when SpaceX acquired the company in June 2026.

**AI does a job for him.** Nomads.com, formerly Nomad List, has a community chat of about 40,000 people (his figure in July 2025; it had 43,252 paid members when he made it free in September 2026), and communities need moderating. He handed that to an AI model. It reads the messages and, when someone breaks the rules, mutes them for a set time, where he would have banned them. He says it's more even-handed than he was.

And there's a fourth thing, which is less a use than a windfall. He told Collison that traffic from ChatGPT had grown in a month from about 4 percent of his Google traffic to about 20 percent. People now ask an AI where to find remote jobs or a headshot tool, and it sends them to him.

## How he works

From his interviews, it comes down to six habits.

1. **Launch small and fast.** Treat every idea as an experiment.
2. **Watch whether people pay.** He tells founders to get real traction, something like $50,000 to $100,000 a month, before trying to scale anything.
3. **Take payment the easy way.** Start with a simple hosted payment link. Build a proper payment system later, if ever.
4. **Keep things running.** Old projects stay on his server. They cost almost nothing and some still earn.
5. **Put the profit somewhere boring.** He recommends low-cost index funds and warns against spending everything you make.
6. **Tell people.** Everything he launches is announced to a very large audience. About 187,000 people get his posts by email, and nearly a million follow him on X.

## What made it possible

- **Ten years of audience-building** before AI arrived. When he launched Photo AI, hundreds of thousands of people already knew his name.
- **Older products that paid the bills,** so an AI experiment could fail without hurting him.
- **A stack simple enough to run alone.**
- **Speed.** He had an avatar app on sale while the craze was still going.

Readers can use the last two straight away. The first two took him a decade.

> **Try this**
>
> **Make a public deadline.** Pieter couldn't finish anything until he promised the internet a launch a month. Tell one person, or your customers, the date your next thing ships.
>
> **Start as a spreadsheet.** Nomad List's first version was a shared Google Sheet. Before you build software, ask whether a sheet, a form or a document would prove the idea.
>
> **Use a payment link.** If you can sell it through a hosted checkout page, don't build a payments system yet.
>
> **Give AI one small, repeating job.** His was moderating a community. Yours might be sorting enquiries, tagging invoices or drafting first replies. Choose a job where a wrong answer is cheap to fix.
>
> **Keep the old things alive.** Before you shut down something that still earns a little and costs almost nothing, ask what it would cost to simply leave it running.

## What could go wrong

**Other people's models.** Photo AI and Interior AI depend on image models and rented computers that he doesn't own. The big AI companies' own image tools get better every few months, and any of them could make a headshot product look unnecessary.

**The file.** One enormous file suits a single expert. It would be hard to hand over, hard for anyone else to check for security problems, and hard to sell. If you aren't Pieter, don't assume it will work for you.

**One person.** Nobody else is involved. If he's ill, bored or distracted, the business stops with him. The long tail of old projects softens that, but it's still a risk.

**The audience.** His distribution is his following on X. He's an outspoken presence there, on politics as well as software, and a business built on one platform and one personality is exposed to both.

**His word.** All of it comes from his own posts and interviews. He has been consistent for a decade, which counts for something, but nobody has audited any of it.

## What to watch

1. **Photo AI's slide.** It has gone from $105,000 a month in March 2026 to $68,000 in November. Does it level off?
2. **Video.** Does the editor he added in August 2026 become a new source of income?
3. **The big labs.** How close do their free image tools get to what he charges for?
4. **Nomads.com without a paywall.** He says he will rely on sponsors. Does the community grow enough to pay its way?
5. **A sale.** We found no sign of one. If he ever sells the AI products, that would be a real change.

The AI isn't really the unusual part of this story. Thousands of people have access to the same models. The unusual part is that he has kept the whole thing small enough to run alone for more than ten years.
