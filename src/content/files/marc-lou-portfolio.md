Hello again, and welcome to File #007. Thank you for reading.

In the thirty days to 11 November 2026, a piece of software called ShipFast made $1,146, according to its creator's own website. That is a perfectly nice amount of money for a side project. It's a strange amount for ShipFast, which by his figures has taken in more than a million dollars, made its creator famous, and was earning over $20,000 a month as recently as last December.

Its creator doesn't seem worried. By the time it faded, he had already built the products that replaced it.

His name is Marc Lou. He is French, he works alone, and he has launched roughly 35 small businesses. By his own count around five have worked. This week's file is about what it looks like to run a business that way: to treat products as experiments, to publish the results, and to move on before a winner dies. It's also about how he now builds, which is mostly by telling an AI what to write. (All figures are in US dollars, and unless we say otherwise they are his own.)

## The short version

- **Who:** Marc Louvion, known online as Marc Lou, a French solo founder who builds in public.
- **What:** a rotating portfolio. ShipFast is a starter kit that saves developers weeks of set-up. CodeFast is a coding course for beginners. DataFast is analytics that tell you which visitors turn into paying customers. TrustMRR is a database of verified startup revenue that has grown into a marketplace for buying and selling small startups.
- **Why it matters:** it's the clearest worked example we know of a portfolio approach, where most things fail, a few pay for everything, and the founder's real skill is knowing when to move.
- **The number to remember:** about five in 35. That's his own estimate of his hit rate.

## The founder

By his own telling, Marc spent years getting nowhere. By his own account, years of earlier projects never earned more than a few thousand dollars a month. His first SaaS peaked at around $3,000 to $4,000 before COVID wiped it out. His public count of how many startups he has built moves around: about 35 in an April 2026 interview, 38 on his site in late October 2026, and 43 on the site when we checked it in November.

In 2021 he took a job. He has written that he was working for the American internet personality Tai Lopez (Remarkable Magazine calls the role marketing; Streakr, quoting him, calls it product manager). In October 2021 he and his wife went back to Bali, where they had lived since 2018. In November he was fired. Remarkable Magazine says he had about $20,000 and no backup plan. He went back to building things.

This time he did two things differently. He kept the products small, so each one took days or weeks to build. And he posted everything he did on X as he did it: the launches, the numbers, the flops.

Remarkable Magazine describes seventeen products in two years. Most went nowhere. Some flopped inside a week. But the audience grew with each one, and people seemed to enjoy watching the flops as much as the wins.

## The product that paid for everything

By the summer of 2023 Marc had noticed something about his own work. Every time he started a new product he built the same things first: the sign-in, the payment page, the emails, the database, the landing page. He had already done that run of small products.

So he packaged it. ShipFast went on sale on 1 September 2023. It is a "boilerplate": a ready-made skeleton of a web app, with all the tedious parts already done, so a developer can start on the idea itself on day one.

Here is why it worked:

- **He was the customer.** He'd felt the pain sixteen times. He knew exactly what to include.
- **His audience was the customer too.** By then tens of thousands of people followed him, and most of them were developers who wanted to ship things quickly.
- **The timing was good.** The tools ShipFast is built on were becoming the default choice for small web projects just as it launched.
- **It was sold once.** Launch prices were $49 and $69, with no subscription.

Streakr, quoting Marc, puts the launch month at about $40,000, and dates the launch to 1 September 2023. An April 2024 Starter Story article was headlined "$528K in 4 months". Marc's own later account puts ShipFast at almost $200,000 by the end of 2023, which covers roughly its first four months. The two can't both be right. Starter Story now marks its figures as "estimated from public sources", and it dates the launch to 23 August 2023 rather than 1 September, so we treat its figure with caution. Streakr notes that it still turns up in search results. Streakr also says Product Hunt named him Maker of the Year. When we checked ShipFast's page on 11 November 2026 it was advertising $100 off for the first 8,460 customers, with 8,447 makers listed, and prices of $199 and $249 (or $299 bundled with CodeFast), against launch prices of $49 and $69. Streakr notes that the number of discounted places left has sat in the low double digits for three years.

For a while it was most of his income. By August 2024 his X profile was advertising "$62K/m".

## The curve

Products that are sold once tend to follow a curve. There's a launch wave. There's a long tail, fed by the founder's audience. And then there's a decline, as everyone who wants one has bought one and competitors pile in.

Marc publishes his revenue, so we can watch the curve happen. These are monthly figures from his own Stripe-connected TrustMRR dashboard, as compiled by Streakr on 13 August 2026:

| Month | ShipFast | CodeFast | DataFast | TrustMRR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| December 2025 | $20,281 | $22,267 | $16,187 | $22,883 |
| March 2026 | $9,463 | $8,268 | $21,306 | $31,921 |
| June 2026 | $3,338 | $9,788 | $21,487 | $28,603 |
| July 2026 | $4,832 | $6,682 | $27,158 | $42,373 |

Read the first column downwards and ShipFast falls by more than 80 percent in six months. Read the last two columns downwards and you can see why he isn't panicking.

Remarkable Magazine, using his public monthly breakdowns, notes that ShipFast went from roughly 40 percent of his income in mid-2025 (his July 2025 post put it at $18,200 of $48,921, a little under 40 percent) to about 3.5 percent by June 2026, when he posted a total of $83,701 for the month and $3,000 of it from ShipFast.

## The move to subscriptions

The first two columns of that table are products you buy once. The second two are products people pay for every month, and they are deliberate.

**DataFast** came out of his own irritation. Ordinary website analytics tell you how many people visited. They don't tell you which of those visitors paid. DataFast connects the two, so a founder can see that, say, a YouTube video brought in $900 and a Reddit post brought in nothing. It took about four months to reach its first $1,000 a month, which for someone with his audience is slow. He kept going. By the end of 2025 he put it at $15,800 a month and growing 14 percent monthly, with close to 1,000 paying customers.

Its marketing is built in. "Users do the marketing by sharing screenshots of their analytics," he wrote.

**TrustMRR** came from a different irritation: people posting fake revenue screenshots. Marc built a site where a founder connects their payment account and the site displays their real revenue, checked hourly against the payment provider. In January 2026 he wrote that he built the first version in 24 hours. In September he put it at six hours, and said he had used Cursor to build it.

It wasn't an instant success in the usual sense. He has written that he tried ten different versions of the idea and that nine failed. The one that worked was a marketplace. If you can prove a small startup's revenue, you can sell it. The marketplace opened in December 2025, and he says a dozen startups changed hands in the first two weeks. On 9 November 2026 he posted that TrustMRR had handled 215 acquisitions that year, including its first $1 million sale, completed in October. Those figures are his own. By July 2026 TrustMRR was his biggest earner, according to the table above.

A third new line appeared in 2026. **Ship or Die** is a paid community he runs with another founder, Jack Friks. Members pay a one-time fee and commit to launching a startup every 30 days, or they're out. Remarkable Magazine, quoting his June 2026 revenue post, lists it at $15,000 that month.

## How he builds now

He has been specific about how AI fits in.

On 7 March 2025 he posted that Cursor's AI agent was "my AI co-founder". (Cursor is a code editor with an AI assistant built in.) He called it his designer, because he uploads pictures of what he wants. He called it his product manager, and also his marketer, since it writes the sales copy straight into the code. Then came the line that matters: "Now with MCP, 90% of my code output is written by AI. All I do is prompt engineering & code review."

MCP is a standard that lets an AI assistant use outside tools and data. In practice it means the AI can look things up and act, and doesn't only write text.

A year later, in an April 2026 interview with Starter Story, he described where that had led. "Right now my setup is the most boring setup ever," he said: a code editor on one side, a chat with an AI on the other, one conversation at a time. He said he had shipped about 300 features on his marketplace in three months, and six new apps so far that year. (The transcript is machine-generated and doesn't name the editor or the model. In a September 2026 newsletter, though, he wrote that he "opened Cursor" to build the first version of TrustMRR.)

What he said next is the useful part for the rest of us. When building gets this cheap, he argued, the measure of a founder doesn't change. "It's still the number of things you ship."

## The honest part: failure

Marc's hit rate is the thing we'd most like you to remember, because he is unusually open about it and most people aren't.

In that same April interview he said he had built 35 startups, that 30 of them "are nothing", and that "the hit rate is about 5". Given his arithmetic, we read that as five products, not five percent. His review of 2025 names names. He launched three new products that year. BioAge failed. ClipMarc failed. The third was still under wraps. That's the same year he made over a million dollars.

His comment on it was dry: "A good reminder that having an audience doesn't guarantee a successful startup!"

In September 2026 he went further. "I have no idea what's going to work," he wrote. ShipFast and TrustMRR "blew up overnight", DataFast was "slow and steady", and "90% of my 36 startups failed".

He reported 2025 income of $1,032,000, 20 percent less than in 2024, and said he wouldn't change anything.

## What to know about the numbers

Marc publishes more than almost anyone, and we've relied on that. There are still four things you should know before you repeat any of his figures.

- **It is all self-reported.** Everything comes from his posts, his newsletter, his website and his dashboards.
- **He owns the verifier.** TrustMRR is the tool he built to prove revenue is real. His own revenue is proven through it, and it's now his biggest product. That doesn't make any number false. It does mean nobody independent has checked.
- **Two of his own sources disagree.** When Streakr compared them in August 2026, his personal page put ShipFast's lifetime revenue at about $1.09 million and TrustMRR showed about $1.27 million, a gap of roughly $180,000 he had not explained. Those pages have moved since, so treat the gap as a warning about the record, not a current figure.
- **Headline and recurring are different things.** In the same August 2026 check, Streakr found his page leading with about $108,000 a month while the dashboard showed about $43,800 of true recurring revenue. The rest is one-time sales, which have to be won again every month. When anyone quotes you a monthly figure, ask which kind it is.

His reported monthly totals through 2026, across an April interview, a June post and his site in late October, have typically been in the high tens of thousands of dollars: about $77,000, $83,701 and $91,100 in those three snapshots.

## The week it went wrong

On 18 October 2024 a developer posted publicly about flaws in ShipFast's code. He said he reported a more serious bug in another of Marc's products privately, and went public when he got no reply. More reports followed, including one showing how to get past ShipFast's paywall.

Marc's first public response was dismissive. Dozens of people try to break his sites every day, he wrote, and they always call it a critical vulnerability. The community didn't like that, and other developers started a public audit of his products.

On 22 October Marc said he had fixed the paywall, and on 24 October he apologised. Reaction was mixed, though some prominent builders, including Pieter Levels, praised the apology. We couldn't confirm whether existing ShipFast buyers received the fixes.

When you have no users you can get away with shipping fast and fixing later. Once thousands of people have paid, some of whom have built their own businesses on your code, you can't.

## What made it possible

- **An audience built over years.** Every launch starts with thousands of developers watching. He says his newsletter passed 51,000 readers in January 2026.
- **Low costs.** He has no employees, and he restarted from Bali, on about $20,000 by Remarkable's account.
- **A tolerance for public failure** that most people simply don't have.
- **Cheap building.** AI writes most of his code (90 percent, he said in March 2025), so the cost of an experiment keeps falling.

The first of those takes years. The other three are choices.

> **Try this**
>
> **Package what you've built three times.** Look back over your last few projects. What did you rebuild from scratch each time? A checklist, a template, a spreadsheet, a bit of code? That could be a product, and the people you already work with are its first customers.
>
> **Set the kill rule before you start.** Decide in advance how long a new idea gets and what it has to earn. Marc tried ten versions of one idea and dropped nine.
>
> **Ask whether a sale is once or monthly.** If everything you sell is bought once, your income resets every month. Start the recurring product while the one-time product is still doing well.
>
> **Count what you ship.** For one month, track how many things you put in front of real customers, however small. That's his measure, and it's a good one.
>
> **Have someone else check your numbers.** If you publish revenue, use a method you don't own, or say clearly that you own it.

## What could go wrong

**The audience is the business.** Every product he has depends on his following on X and his newsletter. If the platform changes or his reach falls, everything falls with it.

**One-time products decay.** ShipFast and CodeFast are both shrinking. The plan depends on DataFast and TrustMRR continuing to grow.

**A marketplace runs on trust.** TrustMRR is only worth something if people believe its numbers. A disputed listing, or a serious question about the owner verifying his own revenue, could damage it quickly.

**Speed against safety.** With AI writing, by his account, 90 percent of the code and one person reviewing it, the October 2024 problem could come back in a bigger form.

**Most things fail.** Thirty of his 35 did. If you copy the method you get his failure rate along with it.

## What to watch

1. **The subscription lines.** Do DataFast and TrustMRR keep growing as ShipFast fades?
2. **Completed deals.** Marc says 215 startups were sold through TrustMRR in 2026 by early November, including one for $1 million. Will anyone outside confirm those deals?
3. **An outside check.** Does anyone independent ever confirm the figures?
4. **Ship or Die.** Is it a lasting business, or another experiment that gets cut?

After he was fired he spent two years building small things that mostly went nowhere. Then he built one thing thousands of people wanted, watched it fade, and had already moved on. About thirty of his products are gone, and he's still shipping new ones.
